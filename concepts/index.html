Three ideas pros use.

Dow Theory tells you which way the market is going. The Piotroski F-Score tells you whether a company is getting financially stronger. The PEG ratio tells you whether its price is fair for its growth. Learn each, then try the calculators.

📈 Dow Theory

Developed from Charles Dow's writings over 100 years ago, it's the foundation of modern technical analysis. The core idea: prices move in trends, and a trend continues until it clearly reverses.

1. The market discounts everythingNews, earnings, expectations and fear are all reflected in the price.
2. Three kinds of trendPrimary (months to years), secondary (corrections of weeks to months, often retracing ⅓–⅔), and minor (days; mostly noise).
3. Primary trends have three phasesAccumulation (smart money buys quietly), public participation (the big move, news turns good) and distribution (the public is excited, smart money sells).
4. Indices must confirm each otherDow compared industrials with transport stocks. A move is stronger when related indices make new highs (or lows) together.
5. Volume must confirm the trendIn an uptrend, volume should rise on up-moves and shrink on pullbacks.
6. A trend lasts until it clearly reversesUptrend = higher highs and higher lows. It ends only when a lower high is followed by a break below the last higher low.
🇮🇳 Using it in India: check the Nifty 50's primary trend first (weekly chart: higher highs and lows?). Look for confirmation from Bank Nifty and the Nifty Midcap / Smallcap indices: if Nifty makes a new high but they don't, the rally is narrow. Watch volume on breakouts. Trade stocks in the direction of the primary trend; treat pullbacks in an uptrend as buying zones, not reversals, until the higher-low structure breaks.
Limitations: signals come late (it confirms trends, it doesn't predict them), and spotting phases is partly subjective.

🧮 Piotroski F-Score

Created by accounting professor Joseph Piotroski (2000), it scores a company from 0 to 9 on nine simple tests of profitability, debt and efficiency, comparing this year with last year. 8–9 = strong and improving, 0–2 = weak. It's especially useful for spotting value stocks that are genuinely getting better (and avoiding value traps).

Find these in the annual P&L, balance sheet and cash-flow statement (e.g. on Screener: Profit & Loss, Balance Sheet, Cash Flows). Use the same units for both years.

⚠️ The F-Score works best for non-financial companies. For banks and NBFCs, use NPA, NIM and capital adequacy instead. It's a quality filter, not a buy signal: combine it with valuation (PEG) and the chart (Dow Theory).

⚖️ PEG ratio

P/E alone can mislead: a P/E of 40 looks expensive, but not if profits grow 40% a year. Popularised by Peter Lynch, PEG = P/E ÷ annual EPS growth (%). It tells you how much you're paying for each unit of growth.

🔗 Putting them together: use Dow Theory to trade with the market's primary trend, the F-Score to keep only companies getting stronger (7+), and PEG to avoid overpaying (ideally under ~1.5). Then run the trade through your pre-trade checklist.
