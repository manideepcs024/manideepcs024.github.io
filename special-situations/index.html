🪔 Muhurat trading

Muhurat trading is a special, symbolic trading session held on Diwali (Lakshmi Puja day), when markets are otherwise closed. It marks the start of the new Samvat (Hindu financial year), a tradition many trading families treat as an auspicious time to make a first purchase.

NSE and BSE announce the exact time every year by circular. It is usually a one-hour session with a short pre-open before it. In past years it was held in the evening; in 2025 it moved to the afternoon. Always check the circular.

🪔📈
✅ It's a real sessionTrades are binding and settle normally (T+1). Equity, F&O and other segments are open, and normal charges and taxes apply.
📉 Thin liquidityVolumes are low and spreads can be wide, so prices can jump on small orders. Use limit orders, never market orders.
🙏 Token, not tradeMost investors do a small, symbolic buy of a quality stock or ETF they'd own anyway. It's a ritual, not a trading opportunity.
📊 HistoryThe session has more often closed green than red, but moves are usually small. Don't read the next year's market into one hour.
⚠️ F&O cautionPositions opened in this session carry over to the next trading day, often after a holiday, so global news can gap them.
🗓️ Mark itFind the time on NSE's website under circulars ("Muhurat trading"), a week or two before Diwali.

📅 Holidays & F&O expiry

The rule: if the expiry day is a trading holiday, the contracts expire on the previous trading day. If that day is also a holiday, it moves back again. Expiry is never pushed forward.

Tap the days that are market holidays this week:
📌 Expiry days todayAfter SEBI's 2025 changes, NSE contracts (Nifty weekly, and monthly index and stock F&O) expire on Tuesday and BSE's (Sensex) on Thursday. Each exchange has only one weekly index expiry. Rules change: confirm on the exchange website.
⏳ Holidays still decayOption prices fall over weekends and holidays because the days pass. Sellers often price it in a day early; buyers holding through a long weekend pay for it.
🌍 Gap riskOn Indian holidays, global markets may still be open. Positions held over long weekends can open with a big gap.
💸 Settlement holidaysT+1 means money/shares settle on the next settlement day. Sell on Friday before a Monday holiday and funds arrive Tuesday.
📦 Physical settlementStock F&O expiring ITM is settled by delivery. A holiday-shifted expiry gives you one day less to close or roll. Plan exits a day early.
🗓️ Saturday sessionsExchanges sometimes hold special sessions, for example when the Union Budget falls on a Saturday. Watch circulars.

🔒 Unlisted shares: pre-IPO hype vs reality

Unlisted shares are shares of companies not traded on NSE/BSE: startups before an IPO, ex-employees' ESOP shares, or large private companies. They're sold through dealers and online platforms and transferred into your demat account off-market. "Buy before the IPO and double your money" is the pitch. Here's what's left out.

🚪 LiquidityNo exchange, no order book. To sell, you need a dealer to find a buyer. That can take weeks, or there may be no buyer at all.
🏷️ Opaque pricingThe dealer quotes a price with a wide buy-sell spread. There's no independent price discovery.
📄 Little informationNo quarterly results or exchange disclosures. You may get only the annual report, months late.
⏸️ Lock-in after IPOWhen the company lists, most pre-IPO shareholders' shares are locked in for 6 months. You can't sell on listing day, even if the stock pops.
📉 IPO may disappointThe IPO may never happen, may get delayed for years, or may be priced below what you paid in the grey market.
🕳️ Counterparty riskFake shares, non-delivery and fly-by-night dealers are real risks. Never pay before the shares are in your demat (or use a trusted escrow).
🧾 TaxLong term only after 24 months (12.5% LTCG, no indexation); earlier gains are taxed at your slab rate. No STT, so no listed-share benefits.
⚖️ Less protectionExchange investor-protection and grievance systems don't cover off-market deals.

🚪 Delisting: when a company leaves the exchange

After delisting, the shares stop trading on the exchange. You remain a shareholder of an unlisted company, but with no easy way to sell. There are two kinds.

✅ Voluntary delisting

The promoter wants to buy out the public and take the company private.

Announcement and board approval. The stock usually jumps on "delisting premium" hopes.
Shareholder approval: public shareholders must vote in favour (special resolution).
Floor price is fixed under SEBI's formula. Shareholders bid (tender) at prices at or above it through reverse book building, or the promoter offers a fixed price at a premium (an option added in 2024).
Discovered price is the price at which the promoter reaches the required holding (90% of shares). The promoter may accept it or make a counter-offer.
Success: shares are bought out and the stock delists. Those who didn't tender get an exit window (1 year) to sell at the same price.
Failure: if the threshold isn't met or the price is rejected, the offer fails, the stock stays listed and usually falls back sharply.

⛔ Compulsory delisting

The exchange throws the company out for serious non-compliance (not filing results, long suspension, etc.).

Usually comes after a long suspension: you already can't trade.
An independent valuer sets a fair value; promoters are required to buy out public shareholders at that value.
The company, its promoters and directors are barred from the markets for 10 years.
In practice, getting paid can be slow or uncertain, especially if promoters have vanished.
💡 Red flags before it happens: delayed results, auditor resignation, pledged promoter shares, ASM/GSM stage tags, trading in "Z"/restricted groups.
